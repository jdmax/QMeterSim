The proton line at high polarization

A proton NMR line is not a fixed shape. As a target polarizes, the line moves up in frequency and its high-frequency edge sharpens, while its area keeps tracking the polarization. This page follows one 5 T target through a spin-up from 24% to 74% and a slow decay from 79%, and shows the model of this behavior that the simulator uses.

The data

Proton event logs from a 5 T polarized target in October 2022: 512-point sweeps of ±400 kHz about 212.882 MHz, each event the average of 2000–4000 sweeps, baseline- and polynomial-subtracted as described on the simulator page. At 05:20 UTC on 9 October the polarization rose from near zero to 74% in eleven minutes, crept up to 79% by 10:10, and then decayed to 74% by 18:10. The example lines below are the eight events of the fast rise, each a single event, and four averages of the later events, binned by polarization.

    Why the line moves

    A polarized sample is magnetized, for protons per unit volume, and each proton sees the applied field plus the field of all the others. Two effects follow, and both grow with .

    The sample's own field

    Averaged over the sample, the field inside differs from the applied field by times a factor set by the shape of the sample (, the demagnetizing factor) and the packing of the beads. For a proton-rich solid such as butanol or ammonia, at full polarization is about 1.2–1.6 mT, or 50–70 kHz in proton frequency, so a shift of a few tens of kHz per unit polarization is the expected size. Positive polarization (moments along the field) raises the field and the frequency.

    Low spin temperature

    At high polarization the spin temperature is a few millikelvin, and the dipolar fields of a proton's neighbours no longer average symmetrically: neighbours aligned with the field are more likely than not. This shifts and skews the line by amounts proportional to (Goldman; Abragam & Goldman), and is why the line sharpens on one side. Reversing reverses both.

    What it means for measuring polarization

    • Integrate, don't read the peak. Polarization is proportional to the area of the line, and that is what a TE calibration transfers. The peak height is not: per unit polarization it grows by about a fifth between 24% and 79% as the line narrows, so a peak-height estimate scaled from a low-polarization line reads high.
    • Leave room for the shift. The line moves by about 25 kHz between TE and full polarization, so the integration range and the polynomial-fit wings need margin on the high-frequency side.
    • Asymmetry is not a phase error. A phase error mixes in the dispersion, which is antisymmetric: it pushes the line up on one side and below zero on the other. The polarization asymmetry keeps the line positive everywhere. Lines that sharpen on one side as the target polarizes are expected.
    • Expect more distortion from the Q-meter. A narrower line is taller for the same area, so at high polarization the Q-meter's nonlinear response distorts it a little more than a fixed-shape model would suggest.

    Try it in the simulator: set protons, change the polarization, and watch the fit-subtracted line move and sharpen.
