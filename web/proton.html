The proton line at high polarization

A proton NMR line is not a fixed shape. As a target polarizes, the line moves away from where it sits at zero polarization and sharpens on that side, while its area keeps tracking the polarization. This page follows a 5 T target through two runs, a rise to 79% in October 2022 and a reversal from +69% to −60% in July 2022, and shows the model of this behavior that the simulator uses.

The data

Proton event logs from a 5 T polarized target in 2022: 512-point sweeps of ±400 kHz about 212.882 MHz, each event the average of 2000–4000 sweeps, baseline- and polynomial-subtracted as described on the simulator page.

  • October, positive. At 05:20 UTC on 9 October the polarization rose from near zero to 74% in eleven minutes, crept up to 79% by 10:10, and then decayed to 74% by 18:10. Example lines: each of the eight events of the fast rise, and four averages of the later events, binned by polarization.
  • July, a reversal. From 01:50 UTC on 3 July the target held +69%. At 14:09 the microwave frequency was moved from 140.12 to 140.46 GHz, to the other side of the electron line. The polarization passed through zero 19 minutes later, reached −55% by about 21:00 and −60% by midday on 4 July, and held there until 6 July. Example lines: the average of the +69% plateau, and averages of the build-up binned by polarization, starting 1.4 hours after the zero crossing (the shaded band ends there). During the reversal

For about 45 minutes after the microwaves moved, the line is not a function of the polarization. It has both signs at once: its low-frequency side turns negative where the −60% line will peak, while the high-frequency peak of the +69% line is still positive, and the logged polarization, the net area, says little about the shape. Each of these lines is close to a sum of the two end lines, within about 15% of its peak, as if parts of the sample reversed before others. A line of both signs cannot come from a single spin temperature, so the model leaves these lines out. It also leaves out the rest of the 1.4 hours after the zero crossing, while the line went on settling: its center of area moved up as the polarization built up more negative, the wrong way for a line that depends on alone.

    Why the line moves

    A polarized sample is magnetized, for protons per unit volume, and each proton sees the applied field plus the field of all the others. Two effects follow, and both grow with .

    The sample's own field

    Averaged over the sample, the field inside differs from the applied field by times a factor set by the shape of the sample (, the demagnetizing factor) and the packing of the beads. For a proton-rich solid such as butanol or ammonia, at full polarization is about 1.2–1.6 mT, or 50–70 kHz in proton frequency, so a shift of a few tens of kHz per unit polarization is the expected size. Positive polarization (moments along the field) raises the field and the frequency.

    Low spin temperature

    At high polarization the spin temperature is a few millikelvin, and the dipolar fields of a proton's neighbours no longer average symmetrically: neighbours aligned with the field are more likely than not. This shifts and skews the line by amounts proportional to (Goldman; Abragam & Goldman), and is why the line sharpens on one side. Negative polarization is a negative spin temperature, and reverses both.

    This picture expects the negative line to be the mirror image of the positive one. The July line is close to it, but at the same size of polarization it moves about half as far from the center and stays broader. The October and July lines at +69% have the same shape, so the difference comes with the sign of the polarization, not with the run. This page does not explain it; the model fits each sign on its own.

    What it means for measuring polarization

    • Integrate, don't read the peak. Polarization is proportional to the area of the line, and that is what a TE calibration transfers. The peak height is not: for the same area the peak is about a fifth taller at 79% than at 24% as the line narrows, and a negative line is lower than a positive one of the same size. A peak-height estimate scaled from a low-polarization line reads high at high positive polarization.
    • Leave room for the shift. Between TE and 79% the center of the line moves up by about 19 kHz and its peak by about 31 kHz; at −60% they move down by about 8 and 16 kHz. The integration range and the polynomial-fit wings need margin on the side the line moves to.
    • Asymmetry is not a phase error. A phase error mixes in the dispersion, which is antisymmetric: it pushes the line up on one side and below zero on the other. The polarization asymmetry keeps a settled line one sign everywhere. Lines that sharpen on one side as the target polarizes are expected.
    • Don't trust the shape during a reversal. While the polarization reverses, the line can have both signs at once. Its area is still the polarization, but a lineshape fit or a peak height is not.
    • Expect more distortion from the Q-meter. A narrower line is taller for the same area, so at high polarization the Q-meter's nonlinear response distorts it a little more than a fixed-shape model would suggest.

    Try it in the simulator at +79% or −60%: change the polarization, and watch the fit-subtracted line move and sharpen.
