The proton line while the polarization moves

A proton NMR line is not a fixed shape. As a target polarizes, the line moves away from where it sits at zero polarization and sharpens on that side, while its area keeps tracking the polarization. And when the polarization moves quickly, the shape also depends on how it got there. This page follows a 5 T target through eight such episodes in 2022, spin-ups, knock-downs and reversals, and shows the model of the line that the simulator uses.

The data

Proton event logs from three runs of a 5 T polarized target: 512-point sweeps of ±400 kHz about 212.882 MHz, each event the average of 1000–4000 sweeps taken in 20 to 80 seconds, baseline- and polynomial-subtracted as described on the simulator page. Most of each run is a plateau. The eight shaded episodes are where the polarization moves; pick one in the table to follow it through the page.

    Through zero

    The two reversals pass through zero differently. In July the line has both signs at once for about 40 minutes: its low-frequency side turns negative where the negative line will peak, while the high-frequency peak of the positive line still stands, and the logged polarization, the net area, says little about the shape. In September the microwaves drive the polarization through zero in 8 minutes, and the line keeps one sign almost throughout: the positive line stretches a long low-frequency tail as it shrinks, and only the sweep nearest zero shows both signs.

    Why the line moves, and why the path matters

    A polarized sample is magnetized, for protons per unit volume, and each proton sees the applied field plus the field of all the others. Two effects follow, and both grow with . A third comes from the sample not being polarized evenly.

    The sample's own field

    Averaged over the sample, the field inside differs from the applied field by times a factor set by the shape of the sample (, the demagnetizing factor) and the packing of the beads. For a proton-rich solid such as butanol or ammonia, at full polarization is about 1.2–1.6 mT, or 50–70 kHz in proton frequency, so a shift of a few tens of kHz per unit polarization is the expected size. Positive polarization (moments along the field) raises the field and the frequency.

    Low spin temperature

    At high polarization the spin temperature is a few millikelvin, and the dipolar fields of a proton's neighbours no longer average symmetrically: neighbours aligned with the field are more likely than not. This shifts and skews the line by amounts proportional to (Goldman; Abragam & Goldman), and is why the line sharpens on one side. Negative polarization is a negative spin temperature, and reverses both.

    An unevenly polarized sample

    The coil sees the whole sample, and the measured line is the sum of the lines of all its parts, each at its own polarization. When the microwaves drive the polarization quickly, some parts lead and others lag. The sum is then broader than the line of an evenly polarized sample with the same area, with extra weight on the side toward zero polarization. Nuclear spin diffusion is far too slow to even this out across millimetres, so the shape stays after the microwaves go off.

    The first two effects expect the negative line to be the mirror image of the positive one, and it nearly is: it moves about as far from the center and its sharp edge is as sharp. Its tail is longer. Every negative line here follows a reversal, so some of that may be the same unevenness; these data cannot say.

    What it means for measuring polarization

    • Integrate, don't read the peak. Polarization is proportional to the area of the line, and that is what a TE calibration transfers. The peak height is not: for the same area the peak is about a seventh taller at 78% than near zero, a negative line gains almost nothing, and a line taken just after a knock-down is lower still.
    • Leave room for the shift. Between TE and 78% the center of the line moves up by about 15 kHz and its peak by about 25 kHz; at −73% they move down by about 12 and 24 kHz. The integration range and the polynomial-fit wings need margin on the side the line moves to.
    • Asymmetry is not a phase error. A phase error mixes in the dispersion, which is antisymmetric: it pushes the line up on one side and below zero on the other. The polarization asymmetry keeps a settled line one sign everywhere. Lines that sharpen on one side as the target polarizes are expected.
    • A moving polarization leaves its mark on the shape. A line taken during or just after a fast change is not the line of a settled target at that polarization. Its area is still the polarization, but a lineshape fit or a peak height calibrated on settled lines is not. Through a reversal the line can have both signs at once.
    • Expect more distortion from the Q-meter. A narrower line is taller for the same area, so at high polarization the Q-meter's nonlinear response distorts it a little more than a fixed-shape model would suggest.

    Try it in the simulator at +78% or −73%: change the polarization, and watch the fit-subtracted line move and sharpen.
