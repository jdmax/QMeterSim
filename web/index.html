Q-Meter Circuit Simulator

The electronic response of a series-tank Q-meter, as used for cw-NMR polarization measurements on polarized targets. An RF sweep drives a tuned LCR tank through a constant-current resistor; the signal is read as a diode (magnitude) output and as a phase-sensitive mixer output against a reference line. Supply, signal, reference and coil (λ/2) cables are all modeled as lossy transmission lines. Based on the MathCAD model of Court, Houlden et al., computed live in your browser with Python.

Analyzing the signal

A Q-meter sweep becomes a polarization in a few steps, as in NMR_Analysis and rgc_offline. Here they run on the simulated phase signal at the polarization set above, with a thermal-equilibrium (TE) signal from the same circuit as the calibration. Because the true polarization is known, you can see how well each method recovers it.

  1. Baseline subtraction. A baseline sweep, taken with the NMR line moved out of the window by shifting the field, is subtracted. Here the baseline is the same circuit with no signal. If the circuit drifts between the two sweeps (cable temperature, tune), a background remains; the drift control changes the tuning capacitance of the signal sweeps to mimic this.
  2. Polynomial fit. A polynomial, usually cubic, is fit to the wings (the sweep away from the line, shaded blue) and subtracted.
  3. Integration. The fit-subtracted points in the integration range (shaded copper) are summed to give the area .
  4. Calibration. At thermal equilibrium the polarization is known from the field and temperature, for protons and for deuterons, with . Then and . This assumes the Q-meter responds linearly. Large signals are compressed, which shows up as an error at high proton polarization.
  5. Deuterons. The asymmetry of the Pake doublet measures the polarization without a TE: the two transitions' intensities are in the ratio and . The quick estimate takes as the ratio of the two peak heights, which is biased because each peak sits on the other transition's plateau. A fit of the full lineshape, as in DeuteronPeakFit, does better, but only if the output is pure absorption: cables rotate the signal's phase and mix in dispersion. The complex fit adds a phase angle to absorb this. Try both before and after pressing the phase plot's Tune button.

The circuit schematic and the model, equation by equation
  • An RF source drives the tank through a supply line and a constant-current resistor Rcc; a reference line carries the source directly to the mixer.
  • The series tank (damping R, tuning C, coil L) has a fourth transmission line between C and L, usually called the λ/2 cable. The signal line connects the tank to the other side of the mixer.
  • Capacitance is in units of the value that tunes the bare tank (no cables) to the Larmor frequency for the chosen species at the given field. Phase is in degrees from ideal tune with zero line length.
  • The optional polarization signal enters as a complex susceptibility multiplying the coil inductance, L = L0(1 + 4πηχ). For protons the lineshape uses approximations from Hill & Hill and values from Seely. For deuterons it is the Pake doublet of Dulya et al., ported from DeuteronPeakFit, with its dispersion as well as its absorption.
  • Polarization has its physical sign: positive is absorptive, as at thermal equilibrium. The notebook's pol is the negative of this, so its default of −0.0027 is +0.27% here.
  • A length of exactly zero is singular in this model, so lengths are clamped to 10−9 λ/2.
  • Lengths are in units of λ/2, so a coil cable of 1 is a true λ/2 line that passes the coil impedance through almost unchanged. Near 0.5 (λ/4) the line makes the coil look capacitive, and the series tank cannot be tuned.

The Tune buttons

Both use the baseline (signal off) and leave cable lengths alone. Diode tune scans for the dip and refines it by bisection. Phase tune first does the diode tune, then picks the root that makes the phase output an extremum at with the same orientation as the ideal zero-length circuit.
